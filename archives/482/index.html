The Late Kissinger: Brilliance, Cunning and Honesty — 'Let History Be the Judge'

Qijie
2023-11-30
Life & Misc › Qijie's Ramblings
Society

Editor’s note: Kissinger Associates said in a statement released on the evening of November 29, New York time, that former US Secretary of State Henry Kissinger has died at the age of 100.

Kissinger was born in Germany in 1923 and received his doctorate from Harvard University in 1954. From 1954 to 1969 he taught in Harvard’s Department of Government and its Center for International Affairs. He worked in the White House from 1969 to 1977, and afterwards served several times as a White House adviser. He was National Security Advisor and Secretary of State in the Nixon administration, Secretary of State in the Ford administration, a member of the President’s Intelligence Advisory Board, and a defense policy adviser, among other posts.

In 1971, Kissinger made a secret visit to China as President Nixon’s advance representative, a historic contribution to the establishment of diplomatic relations between China and the United States. In 1973 he completed the negotiations in Paris that ended the Vietnam War, and for that he shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Le Duc Tho. In 1977 he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom from President Ford. In May 2016 he received the Distinguished Public Service Medal from the US Department of Defense.

Kissinger’s life was a legend in itself. He had a major influence on American foreign policy, and was called “the greatest Secretary of State in American history” by former President Ford. In his political career he also made outstanding contributions to China-US relations.

Yet Nahum Goldmann, a Jewish-American leader and an old friend of the Kissinger family, once assessed him this way: “If his brilliance were 10% less, and his honesty 10% greater, he would be a great man.” In Kissinger: A Biography, the biographer Walter Isaacson presents a politician who is both charismatic and thoroughly beguiling, who knows how to flatter and is often insincere; under his pen, Kissinger becomes more real, more vivid, and more inscrutably mysterious.

Chapter 34, “Kissinger’s Political Legacy: Policy and Personality”

Because people disliked Metternich’s self-satisfaction and rigid conservatism, they often denied even the reality of his achievements.

— Kissinger, A World Restored, 1957

Napoleon once said that Metternich confused policy with intrigue. In both policy and intrigue, Kissinger was an expert; like Metternich, his policy reflected his complex personality.

Kissinger’s greatest characteristic — the groundwork of his personality and of his policy — was his brilliance, something even his harshest critics had to acknowledge. Whether in casual conversation or in a formal meeting, he could marshal the nuances and the opinions of all sides and carry the discussion to a higher level. As Zhou Enlai said in Beijing in 1971, after his first meeting with Kissinger, when they had discussed the world balance: “Dr. Kissinger, you are far too brilliant.”

And the core of his brilliance was his ability to see the connections between different events, and to conceptualize the regular patterns he observed. Like a spider on its web, he could sense — sometimes too keenly — what an action in one corner of the world would set off elsewhere, and how the use of power in one region would ripple into other regions.

In exploring concepts he was intellectually honest, something that surprised even those who considered him a cunning man. He liked to be around quick-witted people who held different philosophical views, constantly challenging their ideas, and he was willing to accept their challenges too.

His critics said that Kissinger’s brilliance showed him to be mainly a tactician rather than a strategist. In other words, he was good at planning the steps needed to accomplish a task, but lacked the vision to draw up grand designs. Leslie Gelb wrote in 1976 that Kissinger was not a Bismarck but rather “a Don Juan of international diplomacy, dashing and charming, leaving his affections everywhere, and in the end surmounting the difficulties and winning the victory.”

In fact, Kissinger was both a strategist and a tactician. The strength of his mind lay in his ability to operate on different levels — from the grand to the minute. His essays contain aphorisms meant to hold true everywhere, and also finely drawn outlines of small events; in his daily work he worried both about the historical forces that sweep everything before them and about trivial slights in officialdom; in his breast he carried grand geostrategic plans — for the Middle East, for détente, for the Soviet-China-US triangle — as well as countless minute tactical bargaining levers that he hoped to try out.

Kissinger’s European-style philosophy of international affairs — a power-oriented realism, or realpolitik — can be traced back to his background. Because he was born in the Weimar Republic and was a victim of the fanatical Nazi ideology, the longing for stability and order had become part of his character. In the environment where he grew up, trust was not easily cultivated, and one did not see good deeds rewarded, so he naturally took a pessimistic view of human nature — and that is precisely the basic premise of realpolitik. He had “the brooding melancholy that people who suffered misfortune in childhood can never shake off for the rest of their lives,” said his old Harvard colleague Stanley Hoffmann. The ghost of Spengler accompanied him all along.

He became suspicious of everything, inclined to manipulate people’s antagonisms rather than appeal to their good side. As he said in a 1945 letter describing survivors of the concentration camps: “They have seen the ugliest side of human nature — who can blame them for being suspicious?” So he came to believe that what determines world order is power, not justice, and that a country fighting for the moral justice it perceives is more dangerous than one acting according to its own interests. One of the lessons he drew from the Jewish Holocaust, as he said in a letter just after the war, was that sometimes “only by lying can you stay alive,” and that “weakness is a synonym for death.”

Before the 1972 summit, Kissinger visited the Kremlin, and Brezhnev took him to see the urns displayed in the hall, polished to a gleam and carefully covered. Brezhnev said the cloths would only be removed before Nixon arrived, to guarantee they were spotless. Kissinger later said this showed that the Soviet soul had always been troubled by a deep insecurity. “But don’t forget,” he added, “insecurity leads to grandiloquence and self-importance.”

Kissinger would probably also admit that his own soul had always been troubled by insecurity, and that he had the well-known flaw of arrogance — perhaps an after-effect of feeling as a child that he was smarter than everyone around him but had no way to show it. Intellectually he was very sure of himself: he took pleasure in the clash of ideas, and welcomed frank challenges to his theories. He was extremely sensitive to slights from others, almost to the point of paranoia, and with colleagues he was often evasive, a trait that grew worse after he happened to be paired with Richard Nixon.

He liked to keep everything secret, and even to not tell the truth. Partly this was a reflection of his insecurity and nervousness, but it also had to do with the policies he pursued. Diplomacy based on moral ideals or international law is easy to proclaim in public, whereas a realist line involving ambiguous compromises and power tricks inevitably requires concealment and deception, because proclaiming it publicly would surely provoke public condemnation. Because Kissinger believed in his heart that his cool calculation of national security interests would not be supported by the public or by Congress, his foreign policy was characterized by concealment and surprise. “If his brilliance were 10% less, and his honesty 10% greater, he would be a great man,” said Nahum Goldmann, a Jewish-American leader and an old friend of the Kissinger family.

Of course, Kissinger’s fondness for secrecy also had to do with Nixon’s personality and wishes. Kissinger reflected — and strengthened — the dark side of the president who promoted him. Both men preferred dramatic, shocking gestures, such as announcing the opening to China; both were eager to control everything and monopolize the glory, and disliked sharing responsibility; both were basically suspicious, and especially distrustful of the bureaucracy. So they liked to operate in the dark, and were especially afraid of leaks.

Kissinger always wanted to keep his colleagues out of things, partly driven by vanity, though most of the time his vanity had a factual basis. Kissinger believed — and there was some truth to it — that in order to build delicate connections and tune a subtle balance, he had to operate through back channels, so as to keep strict control over every aspect of policy. In addition, he felt that if the State Department was kept in the dark, he could negotiate the opening to China better; that if he did not let Thieu know what was going on, he could reach an agreement on South Vietnam more easily; and that if he bypassed Gerard Smith and his SALT experts, he could nail down an arms control agreement with the Soviets.

In these and countless other cases, Kissinger did achieve some remarkable things, but at the same time he planted destructive resentment. His secretive working style may have brought enviable short-term success, but it thickened the atmosphere of suspicion around Nixon, damaged the bureaucracy’s support for the president, and in the end produced an unnecessary backlash against the president’s policies.

Another reflection of Kissinger’s personal insecurity and intellectual self-regard was his urgent desire to win his opponents over, and his deep conviction that he could do it. He had the superb, enchanting skill of a snake charmer, and he was not choosy about whom he charmed. He knew how to satisfy vanity, how to put people on a pedestal, how to provoke quarrels between opponents, and how to gossip behind the backs of mutual friends.

Strangest of all, the list of people he longed to win over embraced almost everyone: from Barry Goldwater to Fulbright, from Norman Mailer to William Buckley, from Haldeman to Morton Halperin. “For a man to want everyone to be captivated by him shows just how far his vanity and insecurity go,” said Polly Kraft, who saw with her own eyes Kissinger working on her husband Joseph year after year.

In Arthur Schlesinger’s words, this trait reflected “the soul of a refugee.” Diane Sawyer also called it “a typical immigrant’s mentality of needing to please people.” A simpler explanation is that Kissinger did it for reasons no different from anyone else’s, though perhaps to a different degree: the longing to be liked and affirmed. He was thin-skinned by nature, and after entering government work he felt especially surrounded and insecure. The eyes in his seemingly sorrowful face betrayed a look urgently in need of recognition.

And Kissinger’s technique usually worked quite well, because he really was attractive, clever and witty. But there was inevitably a negative side: precisely because he wanted to charm so many people, he could not avoid a reputation for being two-faced. “Henry seems to take delight in this roundabout stuff,” said James Schlesinger. “Other people look embarrassed when they lie, but Henry is completely composed, as if striking a ballet arabesque.”

Kissinger once told Averell Harriman that if Harriman stopped attacking Nixon, perhaps the administration would invite him to play some part. Morton Halperin, who overheard this conversation from the side, asked him whether that was really possible. “Henry’s answer was that he hadn’t seriously thought about the question,” Halperin recalled. “When deciding whether to say something, whether it was true or not was not a consideration.”

Kissinger’s charm and beguiling quality, his flattery and his insincerity, were all part of his diplomacy. Take the Middle East as an example: American policy revolved around a process, that is, step-by-step shuttle diplomacy, and shuttle diplomacy in turn revolved around Kissinger’s personality. In Israel and Egypt, and even in Syria and Saudi Arabia, Kissinger could rely on his skill at fawning, pretending to be one of them, slandering their rivals in conversation, and spinning those countries’ leaders around his finger.

At an emotional press conference in Salzburg in 1974, when he feared he might have to resign because the wiretapping affair had come to light, Kissinger looked unusually sorrowful. He said some people claimed he cared more about a stable balance of power than about moral questions. “I would rather hope,” he added, “that when the final account is drawn, people will remember that perhaps some lives were saved, and that some mothers were able to set down the worry in their hearts. But let history be the judge.” Rendering that historical verdict is not easy. The structure of peace Kissinger designed places him alongside Henry Stimson, George Marshall and Dean Acheson as an elder statesman of modern American politics. He was also the pre-eminent negotiator of the 20th century and, together with George Kennan, one of its most influential foreign policy thinkers.

But Kissinger never had an instinctive feel for American values and moral principles — as with Stimson, who always valued integrity over intrigue and idealism over national interest. Nor did he understand that the healthy clamor of American politics and the open decision-making of a democratic society were precisely a display of America’s strength. “Henry is a balance-of-power thinker,” said Lawrence Eagleburger, his closest colleague. “He has a deep belief in stability. That goal is the opposite of the American experience. Americans tend to want to pursue a set of moral principles. Henry has no inner feeling for the American political system; his starting point for basic values and assumptions is also different.”

When Kissinger held power, the foreign policy of the country that had taken him in was at a dangerous moment: having become entangled in South Vietnam through insufficient consideration, America was seeing an isolationist backlash; neither Congress nor the public had any interest in spending money on new weapons, or in confronting the Soviets in parts of the Third World that had little to do with them.

Kissinger brought us into the era of détente, thereby ensuring that competition with the Soviets would stay within controllable limits and that showdowns would be less dangerous. Through his design of a network of linkages, he gave America some diplomatic means to make up for a lack of military resolve. Looking back twenty years later, he could well say: “Perhaps we really did make no small contribution to preserving the integrity of the American body politic when it was on the verge of fundamental collapse.” Some of the initiatives he proposed in the process were full of insight and imagination, some were a bit crude and reckless, some were clever, some were too clever by half. As the only European-style realist to have guided American foreign policy, a man in power unbound by the emotional idealism that fills American history, he sometimes seemed to disregard moral rules entirely. But his mind was clear, and step by step he built a new global balance, preserved American influence after the Vietnam War, and in the end contributed to the end of the Cold War.

Though in every regional crisis he always saw behind it a threat of Moscow’s instigation, real or not, Kissinger was still right to resist the dovish, isolationist forces that tried to abandon competition with the Soviets at the time, and he was also right to resist the pressure from hawks and neoconservatives who wanted to abandon cooperation with the Soviets. As Kennan said in the 1940s — and Kissinger reiterated in the 1970s — the rulers in the Kremlin could prop up their system only by expanding their empire or invoking a foreign threat. Without those opportunities, the Soviet system would eventually fall apart, and so it did.

Hence the fundamental change in America’s postwar foreign policy: for the first time since the Potsdam Conference in 1945, we saw that cooperation and competition with Moscow and Beijing could be part of a strategy of great-power balance. That in itself was a sharp-eyed realist victory on the order of Metternich’s.

This new framework contained a new recognition that American power was limited, but it still believed that America could play a major role in resisting the spread of Soviet influence. Anti-Soviet sentiment not as fierce as that of the conservatives who criticized him, a willingness to intervene beyond the limit most liberals could bear — that was the position Kissinger gave America after the Vietnam War, making sure the pendulum would not swing violently back and forth.

The main contents of this policy were followed for 20 years: containment plus cooperation with Moscow, letting the internal contradictions of the Soviet system work themselves out; a step-by-step approach in the Middle East that kept America playing a major role in the region; a realistic handling of China that produced a more stable global balance than before and gave Washington more room to maneuver. When the Cold War ended, this dose of realism also helped America keep operating in a new global environment based on multiple centers of power and balance.

But the reason Kissinger’s power-oriented realism and focus on national interest later struggled was that it neglected the role of morality too much. The secret bombing of Cambodia and then the invasion, the Christmas bombing of Hanoi, the destabilization of Chile — all of this, plus other crude acts, displayed a disregard for what Americans regard as the historical foundation of their foreign policy: respect for human rights, international law, democracy and other idealistic values. The setbacks Kissinger met as a political figure, and the antagonism he aroused as a person, were both because in many people’s eyes his geopolitical calculations completely ignored morality.

Kissinger’s approach led to a backlash against détente; public sentiment swung back and forth again from Carter’s moralism to Ronald Reagan’s ideological fervor. As a result, no different from Metternich, what Kissinger left us was a brilliance of the moment rather than a solid foundation, not a masterful structure built of brick with no straw mixed in.

For Kissinger, stressing realism and national interest — even when its execution looked cold-blooded — did not mean rejecting moral values. On the contrary, he thought it was the best way to pursue a stable world order, and he believed that this was the supreme moral responsibility, especially in the nuclear age.

At a 1988 gathering of Nobel laureates in Paris he tried to explain the relationship between realism and morality. In a closed-door meeting, someone attacked his power-oriented approach for taking no account of moral standards. The Nobel Peace Prize laureate Adolfo Pérez Esquivel of Argentina accused him of “ethnic cleansing and collective murder.” Kissinger began to talk about his childhood, and the whole room suddenly fell silent.

Kissinger said that more than ten of his relatives had died in the Jewish Holocaust, so he knew a thing or two about the nature of ethnic cleansing. For human rights fighters and anti-war activists, it is not hard to insist that the world must be perfect. But decision-makers who deal with reality can only try to achieve the best that is possible, not the best that can be imagined. How wonderful it would be if military force could be banned from playing a role in world affairs, but he had learned as a child that our world is not perfect. People who are truly responsible for peace are different from bystanders; they cannot risk pure idealism. They must have the courage to handle ambiguous situations and to compromise, in order to achieve the larger goal that can only be reached through every imperfect small step. No side has a patent on morality.

Kissinger believed that the idealistic side of the American spirit was a weak spot for maintaining policy in a disordered world. He was right to a certain extent, but in fact it was also America’s strength. The new global balance that Kissinger and others built in the 1970s — a new global balance that preserved American influence in the post-Vietnam era. But the main reason America won the Cold War was not that it won the contest of military power and influence, but that the values the American system reflected — including the strength its foreign policy drew from relying on the ideals of its people — ultimately proved more attractive.
