Anime Games: Why the Sudden Decline?

Qijie
2024-05-26
Life & Misc › Qijie's Ramblings › Reflections
Economy, Society

Anime games suddenly went into decline, and it turns out the reason is gender conflict.

And that conflict is even bigger than the whole debate about marriage and bride prices.

One second we were in an era that produced Genshin Impact, Tower of Fantasy and Arknights — well-known hits — and now it’s turned into publishers telling players “love it or leave it”, while players don’t put up with it either: quitting, deleting their accounts, unfollowing and turning hostile, all in one breath. Girls’ Frontline, Path to Nowhere, Blue Archive and other games have been blowing up one after another.

Tens of millions in revenue halved overnight, and accounts that people had poured tens of thousands of yuan into and carefully built up are being sold off cheap on Xianyu.

Overall data shows that in January 2024, domestic anime game monthly revenue was already [font color=”#B22222”]down 26%[/font] compared with six months earlier — talk about being thrown back to the bad old days in one stroke.

Why did anime games, once so hot, suddenly decline? What does this have to do with gender? We have to start back in 2019, when the film and TV crowd entered the anime game circle.

As everyone knows, anime games and the idol-fandom film/TV/entertainment circle are almost the two extremes of the entertainment industry. Male players of anime games once made up as much as 80%, while more than 80% of fandom fans are women. Because the customer bases are so utterly different, the content and aesthetics are worlds apart too: the former is hot-blooded, pretty girls and long legs; the latter is singing, acting, “gege is so beautiful”. The two sides originally kept to their own business, and then in 2019, the domestic entertainment industry, which had just been through a boom, was forced to shut down large numbers of offline concerts and shoots because of the pandemic, and plenty of people in the business instantly lost their livelihood. At the same time, the anime game industry was booming.

In 2019, Arknights pulled in as much as 500 million in its first month; a year later, Genshin Impact did $5 billion in revenue over 40 months, putting even NetEase and Tencent on edge. So a huge number of anime game projects were greenlit overnight, and the problem that came with it was a shortage of manpower. What do you do when there aren’t enough people for models, rendering, copywriting and scriptwriting? Wasn’t there plenty of related talent in the film/TV circle right next door? So anime game companies said: gimme that! A mass of people changed careers on the spot.

In early 2021, the founder of the domestic VFX company Mojing Tianhe, which worked on The Wandering Earth, Moon Man and The Volunteers, and founder and CEO of the domestic VFX company MORE VFX, Xu Jian, said in a WeChat Moments post: “Because of the crushing talent grab by the game industry, the film VFX industry is facing the awkward situation of having no one left to hire within the next year.” Let’s set aside how frantic the film/TV circle was; the point is that the anime game industry, by bringing in outside talent, took off again after the relatively quiet 2021. 2022’s Path to Nowhere and 2023’s Honkai: Star Rail and Reverse: 1999 were all born under those conditions. But remember what I said earlier? The film/TV circle is utterly different from the anime game circle in content and aesthetics, so the influx of all that related talent thoroughly changed the anime game ecosystem. Games that had firmly served male players changed their banners one after another and started adding all kinds of film/TV content. A whole series of plotlines that only exist in fandom — shipping, pretty-boy types, domineering CEOs — left traditional anime game players stunned. Many veteran players did express dissatisfaction, but because that kind of plot attracted female customers, it gave hope to anime game companies that had long been thinking about [font color=”#B22222”]how to transform, break the deadlock and break out of their niche[/font]. So similar plotlines spread like a plague, and even many games where male players are the absolute majority had to roll out a few unpopular male characters every so often — whether that actually attracted female players, who knows.

And that’s not all. Starting in 2021, a nationwide special campaign to “clean up the chaos of fandom” (Qinglang) was launched

The operations and management that once ran rampant in fandom migrated into the gaming field as well, and all sorts of fandom brainwashing rhetoric suddenly started hovering over anime games: “I’m character so-and-so’s dog”, “game so-and-so is my problem”… You’d guess the next step is game characters holding concerts. So anime games that had once focused on serving their own users and targeted a male audience [font color=”#B22222”]gradually became a replica of fandom in both content and community ecosystem[/font]. Faced with that, a large number of existing players said: time to get out of here.

Among them, the casual mini-games that are as light as anime games became the big winners. Data shows that in 2023 the domestic mini-game market [font color=”#B22222”]earned 20 billion yuan, up 300% year on year[/font]. Players who quit anime games deserve no small credit for that growth. Meanwhile, dissatisfaction among the remaining players piled up fast, and finally blew up in Girls’ Frontline 2, which opened its beta in December 2023. In this highly anticipated game, a popular character from the previous installment, Xunyan, gets all romantic with an NPC in the story ❤️. Playing it overall felt like watching a TV drama — but who plays anime games to watch TV dramas? So angry players quit in droves, and the result was that a game costing hundreds of millions, which industry insiders predicted could pull in over a billion a month, had its revenue halved, then halved again — now around a few million, almost one percent of the forecast. An entire IP ruined in one go. Girls’ Frontline 2 is just a microcosm of the mass exodus of anime game players right now, and there’s no end in sight to that outflow. All that’s left are a handful of games serving their core base, like Snowbreak: Containment Zone, which, because it took in a lot of heartbroken players, is swimming against the current in this big trend.

Right now the whole anime game circle is stuck between a rock and a hard place. “If there’s a male character, I don’t play” has gradually become a player consensus — meaning that if a publisher dares to put out a set of film/TV-style characters like domineering CEOs and effeminate pretty boys, they’ll just quit and never look back. And if you say, fine, turn the wheel hard — well then you’d have to give up the incremental growth you fought so hard for and do a big reshuffle inside the company, which is just as hard to pull off. Whichever road anime game companies take next, it proves the same eloquent truth: absorbing ready-made talent from an industry with a completely different customer base is not without side effects. Every gift fate hands you has its price quietly marked on the tag.

Alright, that’s about it. Thanks for reading.
