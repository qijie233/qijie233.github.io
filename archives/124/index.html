What Data Should You Analyze for a Website from an SEO Perspective?

Qijie
2023-05-03
Tech Notes
SEO, Website

Most people judge how well a website is doing at SEO by looking straight at keyword rankings in a webmaster tool. That way isn’t necessarily wrong, but it isn’t necessarily accurate either — even though the main goal of SEO is to improve a website’s keyword rankings in search engines. So, from an SEO perspective, what other data can you look at when analyzing a website? This article is about my views on that, and I hope it’s helpful to you.

The three stages a page goes through to enter search

A page usually has to go through three stages — crawling, indexing, and being served — and you can get a rough idea of a website’s SEO by looking at the most important data at each stage. Let’s go through the data worth watching at each stage.

Note: not every page makes it through every stage.

Crawling

For a page to be crawled by search engine spiders, we can work backwards: crawling → the spider discovers the page → the page goes live.

When a page goes live, you can let search engines discover the new URL quickly in one of three ways — active pushing, manual submission, or a Sitemap file — and that leads to crawling.

Crawling is the foundation of a page entering search. Only pages that are crawled normally by search engine spiders can move on to the indexing stage.

At this stage you need to watch whether the crawl volume for your site’s pages is normal — for example, for a site with 1,000 pages, how much crawl volume per day is appropriate?

The factors that affect crawl volume include: page load speed, internal links, external links, site update frequency, and the total number of pages on the site.

Indexing

At the indexing stage, the process is roughly: the spider analyzes the page content → the content meets the inclusion standards → index.

For a page to be indexed, the spider analyzes whether the page’s main content matches its topic and whether the content meets user needs. Next you need to consider the page’s user experience, interactivity, and so on. Once the page content meets the inclusion standards, it can be indexed by search engines.

At this stage you can watch your site’s indexed volume, indexing rate, and page quality.

Being served

For a page to be served, the prerequisite is that it has been indexed by search engines (being included is not the same as being indexed — inclusion is the prerequisite for indexing). Once a page is indexed, to actually be served it needs at least a top-20 keyword ranking before it has any chance of appearing.

At this stage you need to watch the number of effective pages, the exposure participation rate, and user search volume.

Put simply: how many pages on our site have SEO value (as opposed to pages nobody will ever search for, like the usual help guides), how many of those have been indexed by search engines, how many were actually exposed to users, and how much exposure they got.

The exposure participation rate can be simply understood as the ratio of pages ranking in the top 20 — basically only top-20 rankings have any chance of being exposed.

Wrap-up

You could say these three stages are the foundation of doing SEO for a website, because all the optimization that follows builds on top of them.

Analyzing the completeness of the site

Once a site can be crawled, indexed, and served normally (a normal indexing rate and exposure rate), you need to look at it as a whole: what the page structure of the site is like now, and which pages the business needs in order to meet user needs.

Take a local home-renovation company site: it needs a homepage (the main landing page), a design case channel, a 360° VR case channel, a property development channel matching the cases, a designer channel page, a renovation knowledge channel page, a company introduction (mainly to show the company’s strength and certificates), and so on. It’s recommended that the designer, case, property, and construction site pages correspond and link to each other — this kind of multi-page interlinking not only makes pages more relevant and improves user stickiness, but also boosts user trust.

I believe that approach helps users as much as possible; even without SEO, search engines will rate the site higher and bring it more exposure and real traffic.

Optimize every page on the site as if it were the homepage

The homepage is usually what every optimizer treats as the top priority, so the homepage rarely has optimization problems. The problems are on inner pages, especially channel pages.

These channel pages split traffic away from the homepage, and when a channel or other inner page fails to meet user needs, the likely outcome is that users close the page and the traffic is lost.

So you need to watch the completeness of each page and analyze whether the current page still has room for improvement to meet more user needs.

Second, if you want to increase the number of keywords and the rankings for a single page, you need to watch the landing page’s internal and external links — their quantity, relevance, and so on.

Traffic

Honestly, no matter how well the SEO is done, traffic is what deserves the most attention. After all, traffic is what best reflects value. Everyone doing SEO knows in their heart that SEO is a long-term process; if there isn’t much traffic coming from SEO, no company is willing to keep investing in it.

So you need to watch which landing pages carry the key traffic, whether that traffic is well-targeted, and so on.

Conclusion:

That’s what this article covers about the data you often need to watch when analyzing a website from an SEO perspective. From the site going live to acquiring traffic, you roughly need to watch whether pages are crawled normally, page load speed, page quality, the number of pages, the number of effective pages, effective exposure, the number of external links, traffic and traffic precision, and so on. Of course, these are just the data I pay attention to more often; there’s more data worth watching, such as site authority, TDK information, dead links, 404 pages, files, and so on.
