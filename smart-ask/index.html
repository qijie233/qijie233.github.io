In the world of hackers, the kind of answer you get when you ask a technical question depends on how hard the answer is to dig out, and equally on the way you ask the question. This guide is meant to help you improve your questioning technique so that you get the answers you want most.

The first thing you must understand is that hackers care only about hard problems, or about good questions that set their minds working. Otherwise, why would we be here at all? If you have a good question that is worth chewing over and savoring, we will be more than grateful to you. Good questions are a stimulus and a valuable gift: they raise our understanding, and they usually expose problems we had never noticed or thought about before. Among hackers, “Good question!” is a strong and heartfelt compliment.

Although hackers have a bad reputation for despising simple questions and being unfriendly, and it sometimes looks as though we are hostile to newbies and to the ignorant, that is not really how it is.

We do not want to conceal our contempt for a certain sort of people — those who are unwilling to think, or who do not do the things they ought to do before asking. Such people only murder time: they take and never give, they drain our time for nothing, when we could be spending it on more interesting problems or on people more worthy of an answer. We call such people “losers” (and for historical reasons we sometimes spell it “lusers”).

We are to a large extent volunteers, taking time out of busy lives to answer questions, and we are often swamped by them. So we filter topics ruthlessly, and in particular we throw away the characters who look like losers, so as to spend our time more efficiently answering the winners’ questions.

If you find our overbearing attitude objectionable, if it makes you feel wronged, try putting yourself in our shoes. We are not asking you to bow down to us — in fact, most of us like nothing better than a fair trade: as long as you put in a little effort to meet the bare minimum requirements, we will welcome you into our culture. But there is no point in our helping people who will not help themselves. If you cannot accept this kind of “discrimination”, we suggest you spend some money on a commercial technical support contract instead, and stop begging hackers for help.

If you decide to come to us for help, of course you do not want to be regarded as a loser, and still less do you want to become one of the losers. The best way to get an effective answer at once is to ask like a winner — smart, confident, with an approach to solving the problem, who only occasionally needs a little help on one particular point.

= Before You Ask =

Before asking a technical question by e-mail, in a newsgroup or in a chat room, check whether you have done the following:

  1. Read the manual through and try to find the answer yourself.
  2. Look for the answer in the FAQ (a well-maintained FAQ can cover just about everything :)).
  3. Search the Web (personally I recommend google~~~).
  4. Ask a friend around you who is skilled in this area.

When you ask your question, first of all say what you did before this; it will help build your image: you are not a beggar trying to get something for nothing, and you do not want to waste other people’s time. We are all the more willing to answer a questioner who can learn from the answers.

Think it through and prepare your question; a hasty question gets a hasty answer, or no answer at all. The more you show of the effort you put into solving the problem before seeking help, the more substantial the help you will get.

Beware of asking the wrong question. If your question is based on a false assumption, the average hacker (J. Random Hacker) will usually reply with a meaningless literal answer, thinking “stupid question…” to himself, and hoping that you will learn a lesson from the answer to your question (rather than from the answer you wanted).

Never imagine that you are entitled to an answer — you are not. After all, you have not paid anything for this service. You must “earn” your answer yourself, by asking a substantial, interesting, thought-provoking question — one that makes a potential contribution to the community’s experience, rather than merely passively demanding knowledge from others.

On the other hand, showing that you are willing to do something in the process of finding the answer is a very good start. “Can anyone give me a pointer?”, “What is my example missing?” and “What should I check?” are more likely to get a reply than “Please post the exact procedure”, because you make it look as though, so long as someone points you in the right direction, you have the ability and the determination to finish the job.

= When You Ask =

Choose your forum carefully

Be careful in choosing where to ask your question. If you do it the way described below, you are very likely to be ignored or written off as a loser:

  1. Posting your question in a forum that is completely unrelated
  2. Posting a very elementary question in a forum that discusses advanced techniques, or the other way round
  3. Cross-posting to too many different newsgroups

– Write in clear, grammatical, correctly-spelled language

Experience has taught us that a careless writer is usually a careless thinker as well (I would bet on it). Answering a careless person’s question is not worth it; we would rather spend the time elsewhere.

Correct spelling, punctuation and capitalization matter.
More generally, if your question is written like a semi-literate’s, you are very likely to be ignored.

If you are asking in a forum that is not in your native language, small spelling and grammar mistakes are forgivable — but sloppiness in thinking never is (yes, we can tell the difference).

– Use meaningful, specific subject headers

In mailing lists or newsgroups, a subject header of about 50 characters or fewer is your golden opportunity to catch the attention of a seasoned expert. Do not waste that opportunity on babble like “Please help” (to say nothing of off-putting stuff like “HELP!!!!!”). Do not imagine that the depth of your suffering will impress us; do not use spaces in place of a description of the problem, even an extremely brief one.

Stupid question: HELP! My laptop won’t display properly!
Smart question: Mouse cursor deformed under XFree86 4.1, Fooware MV1005 display chipset.

If you ask a question in a reply, remember to change the subject line to show that there is a question inside. A question that looks like “Re: test” or “Re: new bug” is unlikely to attract enough attention. Also, quote and prune the previous text to leave clues for new readers.

– Be precise and informative about your problem

  1. Describe the symptoms carefully and precisely.
  2. Provide the environment in which the problem occurs (machine configuration, operating system, application, and whatever else).
  3. Explain how you researched and understood the problem before asking.
  4. Explain what steps you took to solve it before asking.
  5. List any recent hardware or software changes that might have had an effect.

Try to imagine how a hacker would question you, and give him the answers in advance when you ask.

Simon Tatham has written an excellent short essay entitled “How to Report Bugs Effectively”. I strongly recommend that you read it too.

– Volume is not precision

You need to provide precise and effective information. This does not mean simply dumping tons of error code or data verbatim into your question. If you have a large and complicated test case, trim it down as small as you can.

There are at least three benefits to doing this. First, it shows that you have put effort into simplifying the problem, which increases your chances of getting an answer; second, simplifying the problem increases your chances of getting a useful answer; third, in the process of distilling your bug report, you may find out where the problem lies, or correct it yourself.

– Describe the problem’s symptoms, not your guesses

Telling hackers what you think caused the problem is not much help. (If your reasoning were that effective, would you still need to ask others for help?) So make sure you tell them the symptoms exactly as they are, without adding your own understanding and inferences. Let the hackers do the diagnosis.

Stupid question: I get SIG11 errors over and over again during kernel compiles, and I suspect that some jumper wire is lying across a trace on the motherboard. What is the best way to check for that?
Smart question: My home-built K6/233 system, with an FIC-PA2007 motherboard (VIA Apollo VP2 chipset) and 256MB Corsair PC133 SDRAM, produces SIG11 errors frequently during kernel compiles; the problem appears about 20 minutes after power-on, and never occurs within the first 20 minutes after booting. Rebooting doesn’t help, but shutting down for a night lets it work for another 20 minutes. I have swapped all the memory with no effect. The relevant part of a typical compile log follows…

– Describe your problem’s symptoms in chronological order

The clues most helpful for finding the problem are often the series of operations that came right before it, so your account should include the steps you took and the responses of the computer, up to the point where the problem appeared.

If your account is long (more than four paragraphs), it helps to summarize the problem at the beginning and then give the details in chronological order. That way hackers know what to look for in your account.

– Be explicit about your question

An aimless question is close to an endless black hole of time. The people most able to give you a useful answer are also the busiest people (they are busy because they do most of the work themselves). Such people have little patience with unbounded time sinks, so you could say they have little patience with aimless questions either.

If you state explicitly what you want the respondent to do (give advice, send a piece of code, check your patch, or whatever), you are most likely to get a useful answer. This sets an upper limit on the time and effort required, which makes it easy for the respondent to concentrate on helping you, and that works well. To understand the world in which experts live, think of expertise as an abundant resource and of response time as a scarce one. The less time it takes to solve your problem, the more likely you are to extract an answer from a busy expert.

So it helps a great deal to structure your question so as to minimize the time an expert needs to solve it — and this is usually not the same thing as simplifying the question. Thus, asking “I’d like to understand X better; can you give me a pointer?” is usually better than asking “Can you explain X?”. If your code doesn’t work, asking what is wrong with it is much wiser than asking someone to fix it for you.

– Don’t ask questions you should solve yourself

Hackers are always good at telling which problems you ought to solve yourself, because most of us have solved such problems ourselves. In the same way, these problems have to be dealt with by you, and you will learn something from doing so. You may ask for a hint, but don’t ask for a complete solution.

– Prune pointless queries

Don’t end your question with meaningless phrases like “Can anyone help me?” or “Is there an answer?”. First: if your description of the problem is not very good, asking this makes it even worse. Second: because asking this is superfluous, hackers will be annoyed by it — and will usually express their contempt with a logically correct answer, such as “Yes, someone can help you” or “No, there is no answer.”

– Courtesy never hurts, and sometimes helps

Be courteous; use “please” and “thanks in advance” a lot. Let everyone know that you are grateful for the time they spend helping you for free. And if you have a great many problems you cannot solve, politeness will increase your chances of getting a useful answer.

(We have noticed that, since this guide was published, the only serious defect reported to us by veteran hackers concerns this advice about thanking in advance. Some hackers feel that “thanks in advance” implies that no thanks will follow afterwards. Our recommendation: thank them anyway.)

– Follow up with a brief note on the solution

After the problem is solved, send a note to everyone who helped you, let them know how it was solved, and thank them once again. If the problem attracted wide attention in a newsgroup or mailing list, you should post a follow-up there. The follow-up doesn’t have to be long or profound; a simple “Hi, it turned out to be the network cable! Thanks, everyone — Bill” is better than nothing. In fact, unless the conclusion really has some technical substance to it, a short and sweet summary is better than a long academic paper. Say how the problem was solved, but there is no need to replay the whole troubleshooting process. Besides being polite and giving feedback, such a follow-up helps others searching the mailing list / newsgroup / forum for the complete solution that helped you, which may be very useful to them too. Last, and not least, it gives everyone who helped a sense of satisfaction. If you are not a veteran or a hacker yourself, then trust us: that feeling matters a great deal to the mentors and experts you asked for help. A problem that drags on unresolved is disheartening; hackers are eager to see problems solved. Good deeds are repaid: satisfy that eagerness, and you will taste the sweetness of it the next time you post a new question.

– If you still don’t understand…

If you don’t quite understand the answer, don’t immediately demand an explanation. Go and understand it the way you tried to solve the problem yourself before (using the manual, the FAQ, the Web, the experts around you). If you really do need someone to explain, remember to show that you have already learned something. For example, if I answer you: “It looks like zEntry is stuck; you should clear it first.” — then a very bad follow-up question is: “What is zEntry?” The smart way to ask would be: “Oh~~~ I read the help, but zEntry is only mentioned under the -z and -p options, and neither of them explains it clearly :< do you mean one of those two? Or did I miss something?”

= Think Before You Ask =

Here are a few classic stupid questions, and what hackers are thinking when they refuse to answer:

Q: Where can I find program X?
Q: My program / configuration / SQL statement doesn’t work
Q: I’m having problems with my Windows machine. Can you help?
Q: I’m having problems installing Linux (or X). Can you help?
Q: How can I crack the root account / steal OP privileges / read someone else’s mail?

Q: Where can I find program X?
A: At the same place I found it, you fool — at the other end of a search engine. Good grief! Is there anyone left who can’t use Google?

Q: My program (configuration, SQL statement) doesn’t work
A: That isn’t a question, and I have no interest in finding your real problem for you — not if it takes me twenty questions to dig it out — I have more interesting things to do.

When I see questions of this kind, my reaction is usually one of the following three:

  1. Do you have anything to add to that?
  2. Oh dear, I hope you get it sorted out.
  3. What the hell has that got to do with me?

Q: I’m having problems with my Windows machine. Can you help?
A: Yes. Throw out that Microsoft junk and switch to Linux.

Q: I’m having problems installing Linux (or X). Can you help?
A: No. I would have to lay hands on your machine myself to find the fault. Better go to your local Linux user group and ask for hands-on guidance (you can find a list of user groups here).

Q: How can I crack the root account / steal OP privileges / read someone else’s mail?
A: Wanting to do such a thing shows that you are a despicable scoundrel; looking for a hacker to help you shows that you are an idiot!

= Good Questions, Bad Questions =

Finally, let me give a few examples to show how to ask questions smartly; two ways of asking the same question are placed side by side, one foolish, the other wise.

Stupid question: Where can I find information about the Foonly Flurbamatic?
// This way of asking is simply begging for an “STFW” in reply.

Smart question: I googled for “Foonly Flurbamatic 2600” but got no useful results. Does anyone know where to find information on programming this device?
// This one has already STFW’d, and it looks as if he really is in trouble.

Stupid question: The source I got from the FOO project won’t compile. Why is it so broken?
// He thinks it is all somebody else’s fault, the arrogant git.

Smart question: The FOO project code won’t compile under Nulix 6.2. I’ve read the FAQ, but it says nothing about problems related to Nulix. Here is the transcript of my build; is there anything I did wrong?
// He specified the environment, read the FAQ and pointed out the error, and he did not put the blame for the problem on anyone else. This fellow is worth paying attention to.

Stupid question: My motherboard is having problems. Who will help me?
// The usual reply of J. Random Hacker to this kind of question is: “Right, do you want me to burp you and change your diaper too?”, followed by a press of the delete key.

Smart question: I tried X, Y and Z on the S2464 motherboard to no effect, and then I tried A, B and C. Note the curious phenomenon when I tried C. Clearly there is some shrinkage in the sideband transmission, but the results are unexpected. What are the usual causes of sideband leakage on multiprocessor motherboards? Does anyone have a good idea what tests I should run next to pin down the problem?
// This fellow, from another angle, is worth answering. He has shown the ability to solve problems, instead of sitting around waiting for an answer to fall from the sky.

In the last question, notice the subtle but important difference between “tell me the answer” and “give me a hint and point out what diagnostic work I should do next”. In fact, the latter question stems from a real posting on the Linux kernel mailing list in August 2001. I (Eric) was the person who asked it. I observed this unexplained locking behavior on a Tyan S2464 motherboard, and the members of the list supplied the key information that solved the problem.

By asking the way I did, I gave people something worth chewing on; I made it easy and appealing for people to take part and be drawn in. I showed that I had the same ability as they did, and invited them to explore the matter together with me. I told them about the blind alleys I had already been down, so that they would not waste time on them again, and that is a way of respecting the value of other people’s time. Later, when I thanked everyone and remarked how well this process (the discussion in the mailing list) had worked, a Linux kernel mailing list (lkml) member said that the problem had been solved not because I was a “celebrity” on the list, but because I had asked the question in the right way. We hackers are, in some sense, people with abundant knowledge and little human warmth; I am sure he was right, and that if I had asked like a beggar, then no matter who I was, some people would certainly have been annoyed or would simply have ignored me. He suggested that I write the incident down, to give some guidance to the people who write this guide.

= What If You Can’t Find an Answer =

If you still can’t get an answer, please don’t think that we feel we are unable to help you. Sometimes it is simply that the people who saw your question don’t know the answer. No response does not mean you have been ignored, though admittedly that difference is hard to tell apart.

On the whole, simply re-posting your question is a very bad idea. It will be seen as pointless noise.

You can obtain help through other channels, which are usually better suited to a beginner’s needs. There are many online and local user groups, formed by zealous software enthusiasts (even though they may never have written any software themselves). Usually people set up such groups so that they can help one another and help newbies.

In addition, you can turn to many commercial companies for help, large or small (Red Hat and LinuxCare are two of the most common examples). Don’t be dismayed that you have to pay to get help! After all, if the cylinder gasket on your car engine blows — which is entirely possible — you still have to take it to a repair shop and pay for the repair. Even if the software cost you not a penny, you cannot insist that technical support always be free.

For popular software like Linux, every developer has at least ten thousand users. It is simply impossible for one person to handle the support calls from over ten thousand users. Remember that even if you have to pay for help, what you pay is trivial compared with what you would have to spend to buy comparable software (technical support for closed-source software is usually much more expensive than for open-source software, and less substantial too).
